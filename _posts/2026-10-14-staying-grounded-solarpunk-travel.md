For about seven years now, my partner Claire and I have been running a slow experiment that we eventually started calling [Solarpunk Travel](https://solarpunktravel.org). From the outside it probably looked like a travel blog, or maybe a small nonprofit that never quite got off the ground. But the longer we did it, the more I've come to think of it as something closer to a piece of durational art, in the spirit of something like [Tehching Hsieh's one-year performances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tehching_Hsieh), where the work isn't any single object but the act of living by a rule for a long time and seeing what it does to you.

Our rule was simple: stay on the ground. No flying. Get places by bike, by train, by bus, or on foot, and if you can't get somewhere that way, maybe you don't need to go there.

The meaning of the project changed a lot while we were in the middle of it. It started out as a statement aimed at everyone else, and over time it became more about how we wanted to live ourselves.

### The statement

The idea started as a pretty straightforward protest against overconsumption of energy. Flying is [one](https://ourworldindata.org/travel-carbon-footprint) of the [single](https://thetyee.ca/Opinion/2016/02/17/Climate-Scientist-No-Fly/) [most](https://ourworldindata.org/co2-emissions-from-aviation) energy-intensive things a normal person can do. A cross-country round trip can easily burn through a larger share of your [personal carbon budget](https://hotorcool.org/hc-projects/1-5-degree-lifestyles) than months of everything else you do. And in 2019 there was a growing [flight-free movement](https://flightfree.org/) out of Sweden, people talking about [*flygskam*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_shame), slow travel getting [written up in magazines](https://www.cnn.com/travel/article/train-travel-flying-climate-scn-intl-c2e). We took the pledge that year, inspired in part by climate scientist [Peter Kalmus](https://thetyee.ca/Opinion/2016/02/17/Climate-Scientist-No-Fly/), and eventually even ended up [in](https://www.sierraclub.org/sierra/these-americans-quit-flying-planet) [the](https://www.cnn.com/travel/article/lbg-iyw-flight-free-travel/index.html) magazines ourselves. It felt like we could be part of something.

Then the pandemic hit, and I honestly believed that we were going to come out of it different. People had learned they could work from anywhere, that they didn't need to commute, that the cities were quieter and the air was [cleaner](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/goddard/nasa-satellite-data-show-30-percent-drop-in-air-pollution-over-northeast-u-s/) without all the cars. It didn't seem crazy to think we might redesign life around something like [degrowth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrowth), or at least a little less growth, and that lifestyle changes from people like us would snowball into a systemic change.

So in 2021 we set out on a [solar-powered ebike](/2021/04/ebike-solar-trailer/) [tour of the Pacific Northwest](https://solarpunktravel.org/2021/09/15/a-life-changing-three-months.html), and by that fall we'd sold our car and given away half our stuff so that we could keep going. We were visiting cooperatives, working on farms, and documenting how you could travel in a way that would be possible for *everyone* to do. Our [mission statement](https://solarpunktravel.org/regenerative-travel.html) on the site put it as a question:

> Could everybody in the world live at the level of consumption that I'm living at for 10 generations without destroying the planet?

And along the way, we met a lot of people who were genuinely living that answer. [Farmers on the San Juan Islands](https://solarpunktravel.org/2021/09/15/a-life-changing-three-months.html), [off-grid homesteaders in the New Mexico desert](https://solarpunktravel.org/2022/03/30/winter-in-desert.html), [a solar company in Tucson](https://solarpunktravel.org/2022/02/03/tfs.html) that started out delivering panels by bicycle, bike co-ops in nearly every city we passed through. For the first few months, I thought we were seeing the start of something.

### The realization

But somewhere around the middle of our trip, roughly the winter we spent riding across the Arizona and New Mexico desert, it started to become pretty clear that the world wasn't waking up. Air travel was already rebounding toward [record highs](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2025-releases/2025-01-30-01/). Everybody [went](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/09/16/amazon-jassy-tells-employees-to-return-to-office-five-days-a-week.html) [back](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/03/23/5-years-into-the-remote-work-boom-the-return-to-office-push-is-stronger-than-everheres-why.html) to the office, or at least back to the same consumption patterns they had before. The people we met who were living differently had mostly *always* been living differently. We weren't watching a movement grow so much as finding the people who'd been doing this all along.

Nobody told us we were wrong. It was more of a slow realization, the same way I [noticed people freeze up](/2021/09/carbon-fragility/) whenever I mentioned the environmental part of bike touring. Most people just weren't going to change, and our example wasn't going to be the thing that changed them.

{% include carousel.html images="/assets/images/2026/slow/tucson.jpg,/assets/images/2026/slow/nm.jpg,/assets/images/2026/slow/us.jpg" alt="Photos from riding across the Arizona and New Mexico desert in winter" caption="Winter 2022, crossing the desert, about when the realization set in." %}

So what was the point anymore? Over the second half of the trip and the years that followed, I think the piece split into two different things.

### Part 1: Living morally even if it doesn't change anything

#### The honest math

I want to be honest about the math here, because I think a lot of climate messaging isn't. Me not flying does almost nothing. Airlines set their schedules months in advance, and planes [don't fly completely full](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2025-releases/2025-01-30-01/) anyway, so when I don't buy a seat, the plane almost certainly takes off regardless. At the margin, maybe the fare drops a few dollars and someone else who wasn't going to fly buys the seat instead. My missing demand just gets quietly backfilled by other demand.

And even if you zoom way out, and imagine thousands of people stopping, or even thousands of planes being grounded, it still wouldn't show up in any way that matters. We're already [so](https://climate.copernicus.eu/copernicus-2024-first-year-exceed-15degc-above-pre-industrial-level) [far](https://climate.copernicus.eu/copernicus-2025-course-be-joint-second-warmest-year-november-third-warmest-record) [past](https://www.carbonbrief.org/guest-post-what-the-tiny-remaining-1-5c-carbon-budget-means-for-climate-policy/) [the](https://www.unep.org/resources/emissions-gap-report-2025) [point](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/) where warming was avoidable that the difference between this world and that world would be imperceptible in the temperature record. Aviation is [a few percent](https://ourworldindata.org/co2-emissions-from-aviation) of global emissions. Our individual choices are a rounding error on a rounding error.

Philosophers have actually argued about this exact problem. Derek Parfit listed it among his ["mistakes in moral mathematics"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reasons_and_Persons): the belief that if an act's effects on other people are imperceptible, then the act can't be wrong because of those effects. His thought experiment is the [harmless torturers](https://sites.nd.edu/kristin-shrader-frechette/files/2025/01/ShraderFrechette-ParfitMistakesMoral-1987.pdf), a thousand people who each turn a dial that increases a thousand victims' pain by an amount too small for anyone to notice. No single torturer makes anyone perceptibly worse off, and yet together they've committed torture. Parfit's conclusion is that each one of them is doing something wrong.

I find that pretty convincing, and it's a decent answer to the rounding-error problem. But it still frames the whole thing in terms of outcomes, just outcomes that are spread very thin. So I don't believe not flying saves the planet, and I've made my peace with that. What I want to argue is that it matters for reasons that don't depend on the outcome at all.

#### Fairness in a finite world

But I do think it matters for a different reason, and it's a reason about fairness rather than impact. We live in a world that is resource-constrained. There is only [so much carbon](https://www.carbonbrief.org/guest-post-refining-the-remaining-1-5c-carbon-budget) that can be emitted before things get much worse than they already are, and there's only so much of everything else too. If you divide that up evenly among everyone alive today, and leave something for the people who come after us, each person gets [a share](https://hotorcool.org/hc-projects/1-5-degree-lifestyles). When I consume [much](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/news/climate-change/richest-1-emit-twice-as-much-carbon-as-poorest-50-oxfam-report-73612) [more](https://www.eceee.org/all-news/news/richest-1-account-for-more-carbon-emissions-than-poorest-66-report-says/) than my share, I'm not just doing something "unsustainable" in some abstract sense. I'm effectively taking from someone else. From somebody in a country that will [flood or burn first](https://www.preventionweb.net/publication/climate-change-2023-synthesis-report-summary-policymakers). From my own descendants.

Is that a moral way to live? I don't think it is, even if nobody ever catches me, and even if the outcome is the same either way. Most of us wouldn't pocket money from a stranger's wallet just because we were confident they'd never notice, or because the stranger was going to lose it anyway. So for me the question is less about whether my taking changes the outcome, and more about whether I want to be someone who takes.

#### Acting as if everyone lived this way

When we wrote our mission question, *could everybody in the world live at the level of consumption that I'm living at for 10 generations*, we didn't think of it as anything in particular. But it's basically a restatement of Kant's [categorical imperative](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/):

> Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.

The important thing about Kant's test is that it doesn't ask what will happen because of your specific act. It asks whether the rule you're living by could be *everybody's* rule. And when you apply it to flying, the answer is not ambiguous. A single cross-country round trip comes out to [somewhere between one and two tonnes](https://ourworldindata.org/travel-carbon-footprint) of CO<sub>2</sub>-equivalent per passenger, depending on how you count the effects of emissions at altitude. A fair share for one person's [entire year of consumption](https://hotorcool.org/hc-projects/1-5-degree-lifestyles) by 2030 is about two and a half. "Fly whenever it's convenient" is a maxim that simply can't be universalized. If everyone did it, there'd be nothing left for food or heat or anything else.

That's also why Kant calls it *categorical*. A hypothetical imperative says "if you want X, do Y," and it stops applying the moment you stop wanting X. A categorical one doesn't depend on what you want or on what results. So the objection that "it won't change anything" just isn't relevant to it. For Kant, what matters is whether I'm living by a rule I could defend if everyone lived by it.

Kant has a second formulation that I think fits even better: treat people always as ends in themselves, and [never merely as means](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/). When I burn through my share and somebody else's, the people downwind of that decision, in Bangladesh or in the year 2080, become a means to my convenience, and they don't get any say in it.

Kant's weakness, at least for me, is that it's all so solitary. It pictures morality as a lone person in a study reasoning out universal laws from pure logic. And that's not really how anyone I met on our trip was living.

#### Living rightly without reward

I want to be careful here, because "Indigenous philosophy" isn't one thing, and I'm an outsider learning about these traditions secondhand. But a few ideas that come out of specific Indigenous traditions in North America have helped me more than anything in the Western canon.

The first is the Haudenosaunee [seventh generation principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_generation_sustainability), traditionally attributed to the Great Law of Peace: that decisions made today should be weighed against their effect on people seven generations from now. I'll admit it's a little humbling to realize our "10 generations" question was a rough paraphrase of an idea that's been around for centuries.

The second is what the Potawatomi botanist Robin Wall Kimmerer describes in [*Braiding Sweetgrass*](https://milkweed.org/book/braiding-sweetgrass) as the [Honorable Harvest](https://www.upaya.org/2014/06/guidelines-honorable-harvest/), a set of guidelines for taking from the living world: ask permission, take only what you need, never take more than half, use it respectfully, share it, give thanks, and give something back. What strikes me most is her point that these guidelines aren't written down anywhere, or even consistently spoken of as a whole. They're reinforced in small acts of daily life.

That's what's so different from both Kant and from modern Western life. There's no external enforcement and no reward, and nobody is checking. You live rightly because that's what it means to be a member of your community, and your community includes the land, the animals, the people around you, and the people who haven't been born yet.

This is also the kind of morality we kept running into on the road. The rancher who let us leave our solar trailer by the side of his road. The driver who chased us up a mountain road to return a wheel that had fallen off our trailer. The [near-stranger who gave us a ride out of a wildfire evacuation zone](https://solarpunktravel.org/2022/05/22/wildfire-time.html). None of those people were getting anything out of it. They were just doing what you do.

#### The trouble with homo economicus

Contrast that with the model of a person that most of Western economics is built on, [*homo economicus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_economicus): a rational, self-interested agent who's always maximizing their own utility. Under that model, my decision not to fly isn't noble, it's just irrational. I pay the full cost of the sacrifice, and everyone else shares the tiny benefit. Standard economics would say the correct move is to [free ride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-rider_problem): fly, and let other people worry about the atmosphere.

Scale that logic up and you get Garrett Hardin's [tragedy of the commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy_of_the_commons), the idea that any shared resource will inevitably be destroyed because each individual is better off taking a little more. The atmosphere is the biggest commons there is, so by that story, we're doomed.

But this is where the model starts to fall apart. Elinor Ostrom won a [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/facts/) for showing that real communities manage shared resources successfully all the time, for centuries, through exactly the kind of norms, trust, and mutual obligation that homo economicus is supposed to make impossible. And when sociologists actually tested the free rider hypothesis in the lab, most people [didn't free ride](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/Econ264/papers/Marwell%20Ames%20JPubE%201981.pdf). They contributed to the public good even when it cost them. The group that came closest to behaving like homo economicus? Economics graduate students.

I think that result says a lot. Homo economicus isn't really a description of how people are. It's a description of how we've *taught* people to be, and the more we teach it, the more true it becomes. Every advertisement, every loyalty program, every "treat yourself" is training us to treat life as an optimization problem where the variable being optimized is our own pleasure. If you're homo economicus, taking a cheap flight somewhere nice for a long weekend is a perfectly rational thing to do.

#### Is a moral life a happier one?

The obvious response is: fine, maybe it's more moral, but isn't it just less fun? If everyone's trying to maximize their happiness, and I'm voluntarily giving up things that would make me happy, aren't I just worse off?

I actually believe the opposite. I think living morally leads to a happier life, and I don't think that's just a comforting story.

To start, the evidence that getting more of what you want makes you happier is surprisingly weak. People adapt to almost any improvement in their circumstances and drift back toward where they started, the so-called [hedonic treadmill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedonic_treadmill). A [meta-analysis of more than 250 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/) found that people with more materialistic values consistently report lower well-being. Meanwhile, people who were randomly assigned to [spend money on others](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1150952) ended up happier than people who spent it on themselves.

None of this would have surprised Aristotle, whose idea of [*eudaimonia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudaimonia) is usually translated as "happiness" but really means something closer to flourishing. For Aristotle, you don't become happy and then act well. Acting well is a big part of what being happy *is*.

And then there's the personal evidence, which is admittedly anecdotal. I've written before about [carbon fragility](/2021/09/carbon-fragility/), the defensiveness that people feel when the gap between what they know and how they live gets pointed out. I think living with that gap is uncomfortable, and it takes ongoing effort to keep the [cognitive dissonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_dissonance) out of mind. When we stopped flying, a lot of that went away. The way I lived matched what I believed a lot better, and I didn't have to spend as much energy explaining it to myself.

It's not free. It costs real things, and I'll get to that at the end. But in the same way that I argued in [Philosophical Grind](/2020/05/philosophical-grind/) that we should act as if our actions matter, because that's the only kind of universe where acting could matter anyway, I think we should act as if morality and happiness point in the same direction. In my experience, they mostly do.

#### The goats

Where this got the most concrete for us was with animals.

On Shaw Island, while working on [a farm in the San Juans](https://solarpunktravel.org/2021/09/15/a-life-changing-three-months.html), we took part in slaughtering the meat birds. I'd eaten meat my whole life, and I'd never once been in the room for the part that makes meat possible. After that, we started the slow process of going vegan.

But the thing that really solidified it came a few months later, at [Our Desert Homestead](https://solarpunktravel.org/2022/03/30/winter-in-desert.html), an off-grid farm out past Rio Rancho in New Mexico. One of our jobs there was taking the baby goats away from their mothers so that we could have the milk. That's [standard practice in dairy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9311730/), for goats and for cows, and it's something I'd known in the abstract for years. Doing it ourselves made it a lot harder to ignore. The milk we were drinking was the milk those kids would have been drinking.

It's exactly the same structure as the flying problem. My one glass of milk doesn't change the dairy industry. The honest math says it's a rounding error. But Kant would ask whether I could will that everyone take the baby away so they can have the milk, and Kimmerer would ask whether it was really mine to take in the first place. And I think the answer to both is pretty clear. I can't stop the [industry](https://ourworldindata.org/food-ghg-emissions), but I don't have to be the reason it exists.

### Part 2: The world is big

The other thing that happened is less about morality and more about perception, and it's the part I'm most grateful for.

Effectively, we did a seven-year experiment in believing that crossing the United States is an insanely far distance. Which, it turns out, it is, and it has been for all of human history before the last few generations. When we finished our tour, we [took the train from Albuquerque to Chicago](https://solarpunktravel.org/2022/05/22/wildfire-time.html) and watched the whole thing go by. When we wanted to see the West again, we took the [California Zephyr](https://www.amtrak.com/california-zephyr-train) through the Rockies and the Empire Builder past Glacier, and we got stuck in a blizzard outside Tahoe and stayed an extra few days in Glenwood Springs because there was nothing else to do. [That trip](https://solarpunktravel.org/2023/03/01/amtrak-west-us.html) took a little over two weeks, and I came away with a much better sense of how the West fits together than I ever got from flying over it.

I've come to believe that traveling that far *should* be experienced, rather than slept through on a plane. Looking out the window of a train, or riding a bicycle through someone else's neighborhood, should be how you move through the world. You see the land change and the towns get farther apart and closer together, and you get a sense of how big things actually are.

Our era of magically teleporting to faraway lands devalues all of that. When you can be in Japan tomorrow, everything in between becomes empty space that you're trying to skip. And I'd argue it even devalues the destination. I could go to Japan tomorrow and "experience" something "new," and I'm sure it would be wonderful. But because I've decided that isn't an option for me, I value being in Berkeley more. I value the [hills I can bike to](https://solarpunktravel.org/2026/06/10/transit-to-trails.html). I value the fact that a weekend on the [Coast Starlight to Monterey](https://solarpunktravel.org/2026/06/10/transit-to-trails.html) feels like a real trip.

The same thing happened when we lived in Madison. We moved there after the tour partly to live closer to our families in the Midwest so that we didn't need to fly to see them. And because we'd taken flying off the table, Wisconsin had to become our whole world, and it turned out to be plenty big. We rode out to [Blue Mounds](https://solarpunktravel.org/2024/10/20/hosted-bike-trips-wisconsin.html) so many times we lost count. We [skied across the frozen lake](https://solarpunktravel.org/2024/01/01/madison-crosscountryski.html) and took the bus to the [arboretum](https://arboretum.wisc.edu/) with skis in tow. We spent four days stringing together three different rural bus agencies just to get to the [Apostle Islands](https://solarpunktravel.org/2026/06/10/transit-to-trails.html), a trip that used to be a few hours on the [railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_Central_Railway_%281897%E2%80%931954%29) back in 1900, and by the time we got into our kayaks in the sea caves it felt like a real expedition, even though it was only a few hundred miles from home.

There's something a little [provincial](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/provincial) about all this, and I've come to embrace that word. Provincial used to mean that your world was the size of the place you could reach. I think we've lost something by making everyone's world the size of the whole planet.

### Relenting

The last part is the one I have the most mixed feelings about.

In 2025 we [moved back to California](https://solarpunktravel.org/2025/03/20/we-moved-back-to-california.html) to [start our family](https://solarpunktravel.org/2026/08/25/babys-first-year-on-the-rails.html). It was the right decision for a lot of reasons, but it broke one of the core principles that had held the whole thing together. Our families are in the Midwest. Taking a newborn on a three-day train ride each way to see grandparents isn't really workable, and neither is telling those grandparents that they'll only see their grandchild once every few years.

So we've had to relent on "never flying." We haven't actually gotten on a plane yet, but this Christmas we're going to fly home to the Midwest for the first time since we took the pledge.

{% include carousel.html images="/assets/images/2026/slow/DXmzD8fkk8Y_2.jpg,/assets/images/2026/slow/DTsyKWBFLb0_4.jpg,/assets/images/2026/slow/DUBX4LaFLu3_2.jpg,/assets/images/2026/slow/DIMeV2jyABp_2.jpg,/assets/images/2026/slow/DIMeV2jyABp_5.jpg,/assets/images/2026/slow/DXmzD8fkk8Y_5.jpg" alt="Photos of local hikes, ferry rides, and BART-to-bike trips around Berkeley with our baby" caption="Life on the ground in the Bay Area: Berkeley paths, the ferry, BART to bike to hike." %}

I expect it to feel strange. We've spent years thinking of that trip as a few days on a train, and now it's going to take an afternoon. But the years on the ground have already changed how we're planning it. We're going to be home for three weeks, and we're trying to see every friend and relative we can while we're there. If we're going to spend that much of our share on one trip, we want to get as much out of it as possible, and a long weekend doesn't make sense for a distance that big.

Looking back, the experiment didn't start a movement, and it didn't change anything measurable about the climate. What it did change is how we think about distance, and I expect that part to stick even after we get on the plane.

If you've done your own version of this, flight-free or car-free or just a long stretch of staying put, I'd love to hear what it did to your sense of distance. And if you think the fairness argument falls apart somewhere, I'd like to hear that too.
